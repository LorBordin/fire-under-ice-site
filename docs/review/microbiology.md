# Life without light

Reviewers: Paolo Turrini, then Prof. Andrea Franzetti · Status: draft (not on the site)

Under the ice, nothing can live on sunlight. Yet where volcanic heat and gas reach the cave floor we found microbial mats and biofilms [1], and in places like these life can run on chemical energy instead [2]. In 2026 we sampled them at many sites, at Kverkfjöll and in the Torfa caldera [1].

Why these places matter to microbiologists, what we collected, and what happens to the samples now.

## Dark, poor, volcanic

Scientists call these places DOVEs: dark oligotrophic volcanic ecosystems. Dark, because no light reaches them. Oligotrophic, because they are poor in organic matter. Volcanic, because heat and gas come from below. They are rich in chemical reactants, which makes them good places to study life without sunlight [2, 3].

Without light there is no photosynthesis. Microbes take their energy from chemistry instead, for example by oxidising gases such as hydrogen sulfide, and some build their bodies from CO₂. This way of life is called chemolithoautotrophy [2, 4].

In our caves the range is extreme. The air stays at 1–4 °C, next to ice at melting point, while the ground at the fumarole vents reached 94.5 °C, boiling point at about 1700 m. Our gas detectors found hydrogen, methane, hydrogen sulfide and carbon dioxide. Heat-loving (thermophilic), acid-loving (acidophilic) and cold-loving (psychrophilic) microbes can live side by side [1].

Sulfur-rich glaciovolcanic caves are rare. One of the few studied in detail is the fumarole ice cave on Mount Meager, Canada, the only known glaciovolcanic cave system in North America that steadily releases sulfur-rich gases. There, microbes that oxidise sulfur and microbes that reduce sulfate point to life on chemical energy [4]. At Kverkfjöll itself, a hot spring fed by the ice hosts communities that live on volcanic sulfur [5].

## What we sampled in 2026

At Kverkfjöll we sampled near fumaroles and boiling pools inside the caves. We took sediment in triplicate at increasing distances from the vents, following the drop in temperature; sediment from the rims of boiling pools; biofilm from ice walls in well-ventilated spots; and hot water from a large pool fed by a methane-rich fumarole. This material was collected with the agreement of Vatnajökull National Park [1].

At the bottom of the Encelado cauldron, a spring at about 74 °C, with a flow of many litres per second, melts a lake under the glacier. We sampled around it [1].

In Freyja Hellir, in the Torfa caldera, fumaroles and hot springs at different distances from the entrance host soft, creamy, coloured microbial mats. Outside the cave, at the edge of an acidic fumarole rich in hydrogen sulfide, we sampled snottites: jelly-like microbial colonies that hang like small stalactites. Along a thermal stream we sampled filamentous communities [1].

## What comes next

The samples are waiting for DNA sequencing and metagenomic analysis, in a collaboration with Prof. Andrea Franzetti, a glacial microbiologist at the University of Milano-Bicocca. In the team, sampling is led by Paolo Turrini, PhD in microbiology [1].

Why it matters. Life that runs on chemistry in the dark, where ice meets volcanic heat, is a model for habitats that may once have existed on Mars [6]. Microbes adapted to heat, acid or cold may also resemble early life on Earth, and their enzymes, which keep working in such conditions, interest biotechnology [1].
